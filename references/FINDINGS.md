## aarts_2006 — Driving speed and the risk of road crashes: A review
**Aarts & van Schagen (2006)** · *Accident Analysis & Prevention, 38(2), 215–224*

- Speed affects both crash **probability** and crash **severity** independently.
- At the **individual vehicle level**, the speed–crash rate relationship is **exponential**: crash risk grows rapidly as a vehicle's speed exceeds the mean flow speed.
- At the **road section level**, a **power function** applies (consistent with the Nilsson Power Model): injury accidents ∝ v², fatal accidents ∝ v³–v⁴.
- **Speed dispersion** also matters: vehicles travelling much faster than surrounding traffic have consistently higher crash rates across all reviewed studies.
- The speed–crash rate relationship is steeper on minor/urban roads than on major roads, but remains significant on motorways.
- No study found that higher speed reduces crash rate; the direction is unambiguous across all reviewed evidence.

## aarts_2015 — Benchmarking road safety performance by grouping local territories: A study in the Netherlands
**Aarts & Houwing (2015)** · *Transportation Research Part A, 74, 174–185*

- Urbanisation is the single most important structural factor explaining differences in road safety performance between Dutch municipalities.
- Fair benchmarking requires grouping comparable territories; raw comparisons can be misleading.
- Rural territories benefit most from additional structural variables (age distribution, road network) in benchmarking groups.
- The Netherlands performs strongly in international road safety comparisons; sub-national variation is however substantial.
- Primarily a methodology paper on benchmarking; provides context for interpreting NL road safety statistics.

## bauernschuster_2021 — Tempolimit 130 auf Autobahnen: Eine evidenzbasierte Diskussion der Auswirkungen
**Bauernschuster & Traxler (2021)** · *Perspektiven der Wirtschaftspolitik, 22(2), 86–102* · German-language

- Circa **70% of German Autobahn sections** have no speed limit — an international outlier alongside Afghanistan, Burundi, North Korea and Somalia.
- Data quality is poor and causal evidence scarce, but the available evidence points to net benefits from introducing a 130 km/h limit.
- **Traffic safety:** based on the Power Model and international studies, a 130 km/h limit would reduce fatalities and serious injuries; exact numbers are uncertain due to data limitations.
- **CO₂ and emissions:** a limit would reduce fuel consumption and greenhouse gas emissions; magnitude estimates vary.
- **Travel time losses:** a 130 km/h limit would cause travel time increases, concentrated on drivers who currently travel above 130 km/h.
- More than **50% of the German population** consistently supports a general 130 km/h limit in surveys.
- Conclusion: societal benefits of a limit likely outweigh the costs; calls for evidence-based transport policy.

## bauernschuster_2022 — Speed limit enforcement and road safety
**Bauernschuster & Rekers (2022)** · *Journal of Public Economics, 209*

- Studies German **"Blitzmarathon"** — one-day massive speed limit monitoring operations (SLMO) with accompanying media campaigns.
- **Traffic accidents fell by 7.5%** and slightly injured by 8.5% on SLMO days compared to regular days.
- Effects on severely and fatally injured were similar in magnitude but statistically insignificant.
- Key finding: **effects vanish immediately** after the SLMO day ends — the speed reduction is entirely driven by increased detection probability, not by lasting behavioural change.
- Information campaigns about the dangers of speeding alone do **not** produce sustained behavioural change.
- Drivers react to visibility of enforcement and increased detection probability, not to information per se.
- Directly relevant: confirms that German Autobahn drivers do speed less when enforcement is present, implying they routinely drive above safe speeds when it is absent.

## bertini_2006 — Dynamics of Variable Speed Limit System Surrounding Bottleneck on German Autobahn
**Bertini, Boice & Bogenberger (2006)** · *Transportation Research Record, 1978, 149–159*

- Empirical study of the VSL system on **Autobahn A9** near Munich (18-km section with overhead dynamic message signs).
- VSL systems in Germany have decreased crash rates by **up to 20%** (cited from prior literature).
- The A9 VSL system successfully reduced speeds before bottleneck activation; drivers responded to warnings and the road continued flowing at 30–40 km/h during congestion.
- Strong correlation found between VSL display and actual traffic dynamics — the system functions as designed.
- Suggests that mandatory speed limits (even temporary ones) substantially improve safety outcomes on sections that are otherwise unrestricted.

## doecke_2018 — Safe speed limits for a safe system: The relationship between speed limit and fatal crash rate for different crash types
**Doecke, Kloeden, Dutschke & Baldock (2018)** · *Traffic Injury Prevention*

- Uses Australian police-reported crash data to calculate **fatal crash rate by speed limit and crash type**.
- Found a **positive exponential relationship** between speed limit and fatality rate — consistent with the Power Model.
- Example 'safe' speed limits (defined at a 1-in-100 fatal crash threshold):
  - Pedestrian crashes: **40 km/h**
  - Head-on crashes: **50 km/h**
  - Fixed object crashes: **60 km/h**
  - Right-angle, right-turn, rollover: **80 km/h**
  - Rear-end crashes: **110 km/h or more**
- Speed zones of **100 km/h or more** only meet Safe System objectives where all crash types except rear-end are exceedingly rare — i.e. on a high-standard restricted-access highway with safe roadside design.
- Directly relevant: provides empirical thresholds for what constitutes a 'safe' speed limit by crash context.

## elvik_2010 — A restatement of the case for speed limits
**Elvik (2010)** · *Transport Policy, 17(3), 196–204*

- Driver speed choice is **not objectively rational** from a societal welfare perspective, even if individually subjectively rational.
- Three reasons speed limits are necessary: (1) **externalities** — high-speed driving imposes risk on others not factored into individual choice; (2) **misperception of risk** — drivers systematically underestimate crash risk at high speeds; (3) **coordination failure** — heterogeneous speed preferences create dangerous speed differentials.
- Speed limits serve as a coordination mechanism: they reduce both the mean and variance of speed distributions, both of which reduce crash risk.
- Explicitly discusses the **German Autobahn** unrestricted sections as the last major remnant of unregulated speed, and argues they are an exception that does not disprove the case for limits.
- Conclusion: free speed choice does **not** produce socially optimal outcomes; regulation is necessary.

## elvik_2013 — A re-parameterisation of the Power Model of the relationship between the speed of traffic and the number of accidents and accident victims
**Elvik (2013)** · *Accident Analysis & Prevention, 50, 854–860*

- Re-analysis of the Power Model based on 115 studies with 526 estimates.
- Key finding: the **exponent is not fixed** — it depends on initial speed. The effect of a given relative speed change is **largest when initial speed is highest**.
- Exponents for rural roads/freeways (most relevant for motorways):
  - Fatal accidents: **4.1** (95% CI: 2.9–5.3)
  - Fatalities: **4.6**
  - Serious injury accidents: **2.6**
  - Injury accidents overall: **1.6**
- For urban/residential roads, exponents are lower (fatal accidents: 2.6), but for motorways/freeways the original Nilsson exponents are confirmed or exceeded.
- Implication: the Power Model **underestimates** the effect at high initial speeds (e.g. Autobahn); speed reductions from 160→130 km/h have a larger proportional safety benefit than from 50→40 km/h.

## elvik_2019 — Updated estimates of the relationship between speed and road safety at the aggregate and individual levels
**Elvik, Vadeby, Hels & van Schagen (2019)** · *Accident Analysis & Prevention, 123, 114–122*

- Meta-analysis of studies **published after 2000** using data fully or partly from the post-2000 period.
- Confirms: **the relationship between speed and road safety remains strong** — modern vehicle safety technology has not made speed less important.
- Both the Power Model and the Exponential Model fit the data well.
- The relationship between speed and accident risk is **the same at the individual driver level as at the aggregate level** (mean speed of traffic).
- Summary: new cars are safer, but their higher speed performance offsets safety gains; speed still kills at the same rate relative to the Power Model predictions.
- Provides the most up-to-date validation of the Power Model using recent data from multiple countries.

## goldenbeld_2005 — The effects of speed enforcement with mobile radar on speed and accidents
**Goldenbeld & van Schagen (2005)** · *Accident Analysis & Prevention, 37(6), 1135–1144*

- Evaluation of 5 years (1998–2002) of targeted speed enforcement on **rural non-motorway roads in the Dutch province of Friesland**.
- Mean speed and proportion of speed limit violators **decreased significantly** over the enforcement period; largest decreases in year 1 and year 4 (when enforcement was intensified).
- **Spillover effects** observed: comparable roads not subject to targeted enforcement also showed speed reductions.
- Best estimate: enforcement led to a **21% reduction** in both injury accidents and serious casualties.
- Enforcement effects are sustained over multiple years — unlike the one-day SLMO campaigns (Bauernschuster 2022), sustained enforcement produces lasting behaviour change.
- Confirms that speed enforcement is one of the most cost-effective road safety interventions in the Netherlands.

## hermans_2009 — Benchmarking road safety: Lessons to learn from a data envelopment analysis
**Hermans, Brijs, Wets & Vanhoof (2009)** · *Accident Analysis & Prevention, 41(1), 174–182*

- Data envelopment analysis (DEA) applied to **21 European countries** across 6 road safety performance indicator domains: alcohol/drugs, speed, protective systems, vehicle, infrastructure, trauma management.
- **SUN countries** (Sweden, United Kingdom, Netherlands) consistently rank as top performers and serve as benchmarks for others.
- **Speed** is identified as one of the most critical and actionable domains for policy intervention.
- Country-specific policy recommendations produced; countries with poor speed-domain performance should prioritise enforcement and limit-setting.
- The Netherlands is a benchmark country for road safety management — relevant baseline for comparing with Germany.

## johansson_1996 — Speed limitation and motorway casualties: A time series count data regression approach
**Johansson (1996)** · *Accident Analysis & Prevention, 28(1), 73–87*

- Time series analysis of the effect of a **lowered speed limit on Swedish motorway casualties** using Poisson and negative binomial count data regression models.
- Models account for both overdispersion and serial correlation in accident counts.
- Finding: **speed limit reduction decreased the number of accidents** involving minor injuries and vehicle damage on Swedish motorways.
- Models allowing for serial correlation showed the best forecasting performance — methodological contribution as well as substantive finding.
- Directly relevant: provides empirical evidence from Scandinavian motorways that speed limit reductions reduce crash counts, supporting the case for limits on German Autobahn.

## manner_2013 — Analyzing the severity of accidents on the German Autobahn
**Manner & Wünsch-Ziegler (2013)** · *Accident Analysis & Prevention, 57, 40–48*

- Dataset: North Rhine-Westphalia Autobahn, **2009–2011**, multinomial logit and random parameter model.
- Accident severity categories: fatal / severe injury / light injury / property damage.
- **Intelligent traffic control systems** (variable speed limits, dynamic signs) significantly **reduce accident severity** — direct evidence that enforced speed limits improve outcomes.
- Accidents during **daylight** and at **interchanges/construction sites** are less severe.
- Collisions with **roadside objects**, involving **pedestrians or motorcycles**, or in **poor visibility** conditions tend to be more severe.
- Gender and age of driver affect severity outcomes.
- Directly relevant: shows on actual Autobahn data that VSL systems (a form of speed restriction) reduce accident severity.

## nilsson_2004 — Traffic Safety Dimensions and the Power Model to Describe the Effect of Speed on Safety
**Nilsson (2004)** · *Doctoral Thesis, Lund University, Bulletin 221*

- The foundational reference establishing the **Power Model** of speed and safety.
- Core equations (if mean speed changes from v₁ to v₂):
  - Injury accidents: A₂ = A₁ × (v₂/v₁)²
  - Fatal accidents: F₂ = F₁ × (v₂/v₁)³
  - Fatalities: D₂ = D₁ × (v₂/v₁)⁴
- Model validated cross-sectionally and confirmed for injury accidents and fatal accidents; for fatalities the model **underestimates** the true effect.
- Validated using Swedish speed limit experiments from 1967 onwards and international data.
- Practical implication: a **10% speed increase → ~46% increase in fatalities** (1.1⁴ ≈ 1.46).

## vadeby_2018 — Traffic safety effects of new speed limits in Sweden
**Vadeby & Forsman (2018)** · *Accident Analysis & Prevention, 114, 34–39*

- Natural experiment: Sweden changed speed limits on ~20,500 km of rural roads in 2008–2009 (both increases and decreases).
- **Reductions from 90→80 km/h:** fatalities decreased by **14 per year**; no significant change in seriously injured.
- **Increases to 120 km/h on motorways:** seriously injured increased by **~15 per year**; no significant change in deaths.
- **Reductions from 110→100 km/h on 2+1 roads:** seriously injured decreased by **~16 per year**.
- A 10 km/h reduction in speed limit led to a 2–3 km/h decrease in mean speed; a 10 km/h increase led to a 3 km/h increase.
- In total, ~**17 lives per year saved** across the entire network where speed limits changed.
- Directly relevant: shows that **raising motorway speed limits increases serious injuries**, and lowering them saves lives — a real-world validation of the Power Model.

## wegman_2008 — Advancing sustainable safety: National road safety outlook for The Netherlands for 2005–2020
**Wegman, Aarts & Bax (2008)** · *Safety Science, 46(2), 323–343*

- Overview of the Dutch **Sustainable Safety** programme and its updated vision for 2005–2020.
- The Netherlands tops the world in lowest road fatalities per capita — achieved through a systematic approach combining speed limits, infrastructure design ("forgiving roads"), enforcement, and vehicle standards.
- Five principles of Sustainable Safety: functionality of roads, **homogeneity of masses/speed/direction**, predictability, forgivingness, state awareness.
- Speed homogeneity is explicitly identified as a core safety principle: mixing high and low speed vehicles on the same road is inherently dangerous.
- **Cost of road crashes in NL in 2003: €12.3 billion (2.6% of GNP)** — used to argue for ambitious safety targets.
- Vision Zero and Safe System principles underpin the Dutch approach; speed management is central.
- Provides institutional context: NL's strong motorway safety record is a direct result of sustained speed management — the inverse of the German unrestricted Autobahn policy.

## weikl_2013 — Traffic Management Effects of Variable Speed Limit System on a German Autobahn: Empirical Assessment Before and After System Implementation
**Weikl, Bogenberger & Bertini (2013)** · *Transportation Research Record, 2380, 48–60*

- With-and-without empirical study of the VSL system on **Autobahn A99** near Munich (16.3-km, three-lane section).
- Unlike most VSL studies, this had pre-installation data, enabling a true before/after comparison.
- VSL system produced **more homogeneous flow across lanes**: bottleneck flow reductions were balanced across all lanes with VSL active; without VSL, reductions concentrated in centre and shoulder lanes.
- **Safety gain comes at a small capacity cost**: VSL reduces the fastest drivers, homogenising flow but slightly lowering peak throughput.
- Prior literature cited: German VSL systems have decreased crash rates by **up to 20%**.
- Confirms that variable speed enforcement on the Autobahn improves safety outcomes; the unrestricted baseline is less safe.
