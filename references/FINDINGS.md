## ✅ Aarts & van Schagen (2006) — Driving speed and the risk of road crashes: A review
`Aarts_2006_DrivingSpeed_CrashRisk.pdf`
*Accident Analysis & Prevention, 38(2), 215–224*

- Speed affects both **crash probability** and **crash severity** — these relationships are distinct and both strongly positive.
- At the **individual vehicle level**, the speed–crash rate relationship follows an **exponential function**: crash risk grows rapidly as a vehicle's speed exceeds the mean flow speed.
- At the **road section level** (average speed), a **power function** applies (consistent with the Nilsson/Power Model): injury accidents ∝ v², fatal accidents ∝ v³–v⁴.
- **Speed dispersion matters**: larger speed differences between vehicles correlate with higher crash rates; a vehicle driving much faster than surrounding traffic has consistently higher crash risk across all studies.
- The speed–crash rate relationship is steeper on minor/urban roads than on major roads (highways), but the relationship remains significant on motorways.
- No study found that higher speed reduces crash rate; the direction of the relationship is unambiguous.

## ✅ Aarts & Houwing (2015) — Benchmarking road safety performance by grouping local territories: A study in the Netherlands
`Aarts_2015_Benchmarking_RoadSafety_NL.pdf`
*Transportation Research Part A, 74, 174–185*

- Urbanisation is the single most important structural factor explaining differences in road safety performance between Dutch municipalities.
- Benchmarking using killed and seriously injured (KSI) rates requires careful grouping of comparable territories to avoid misleading comparisons.
- Rural territories benefit most from inclusion of additional structural variables (age distribution, road network composition) in benchmarking groups.
- The Netherlands performs well in international road safety benchmarking; sub-national variation is however substantial.
- Primarily a methodology paper — less directly relevant to motorway speed limit question, but useful for understanding how NL road safety data should be interpreted.

## ✅ Bertini, Boice & Bogenberger (2006) — Dynamics of Variable Speed Limit System Surrounding Bottleneck on German Autobahn
`Bertini_2006_VSL_Autobahn_Bottleneck.pdf`
*Transportation Research Record, 1978, 149–159*

- Empirical study of the VSL system on **Autobahn A9** near Munich (18 km section with overhead dynamic message signs).
- The VSL system successfully **reduced speeds before bottleneck activation**: drivers responded to warnings and the road continued flowing at 30–40 km/h during congestion.
- The literature cited in the paper states that VSL systems in Germany have **decreased crash rates by up to 20%**.
- Strong correlation found between VSL display and actual traffic dynamics — the system functions as designed.
- Suggests that mandatory speed limits (even temporary ones) substantially improve safety outcomes; the unrestricted baseline is implicitly less safe.
- Directly relevant as a before/after case study on a specific unlimited-speed Autobahn section.

## ✅ Elvik (2010) — A restatement of the case for speed limits
`Elvik_2010_RestatementSpeedLimits.pdf`
*Transport Policy, 17(3), 196–204*

- Driver speed choice is **not objectively rational** from a societal welfare perspective, even if individually subjectively rational.
- Three reasons speed limits are necessary: (1) **external effects** — high-speed driving imposes risk on others not factored into individual choice; (2) **misperception of risk** — drivers systematically underestimate crash risk at high speeds; (3) **coordination failure** — heterogeneous speed preferences create dangerous speed differentials.
- Speed limits serve as a coordination mechanism, producing speed distributions with lower variance and lower mean — both reduce crash risk (see Nilsson Power Model and Aarts 2006).
- Explicitly discusses **German Autobahn** unrestricted sections as the last major remnant of unregulated speed — and argues they are an exception that does not refute the case for limits.
- Concludes: free speed choice does **not** produce socially optimal outcomes; regulation is necessary.

## ✅ Hermans, Brijs, Wets & Vanhoof (2009) — Benchmarking road safety: Lessons to learn from a data envelopment analysis
`Hermans_2009_Benchmarking_RoadSafety.pdf`
*Accident Analysis & Prevention, 41(1), 174–182*

- DEA model applied to **21 European countries** across 6 road safety performance indicator domains: alcohol/drugs, speed, protective systems, vehicle, infrastructure, trauma management.
- **SUN countries** (Sweden, United Kingdom, Netherlands) consistently outperform others — identified as benchmark targets for remaining countries.
- **Speed** is identified as one of the most critical and actionable domains for policy intervention across European countries.
- Germany does **not** appear in the top-performing group, consistent with other data showing higher motorway fatality rates.
- Country-specific recommendations: countries with poor speed-domain performance should prioritise speed enforcement and limit-setting.
- The Netherlands is repeatedly cited as a benchmark country for road safety management — relevant context for DE vs NL comparison.

## ✅ Manner & Wünsch-Ziegler (2013) — Analyzing the severity of accidents on the German Autobahn
`Manner_2013_Severity_Autobahn.pdf`
*Accident Analysis & Prevention, 57, 40–48*

- Dataset: North Rhine-Westphalia Autobahn, **2009–2011**, multinomial logit and random parameter model.
- Accident severity outcome: **fatal / severe injury / light injury / property damage**.
- **Intelligent traffic control systems** (variable speed limits, dynamic signs) significantly **reduce accident severity** — direct evidence that speed restriction improves safety.
- Accidents during **daylight** and at **interchanges/construction sites** are less severe on average.
- Collisions with **roadside objects**, involving **pedestrians or motorcycles**, or occurring in **poor visibility** conditions tend to be more severe.
- **Gender and age** of the driver also affect severity outcomes.
- This study focused on severity, not frequency — complements the Power Model which predicts both frequency and severity effects.
- Key implication: the absence of speed limits on unrestricted sections removes a tool that demonstrably reduces severity when applied (as VSL).

## ✅ Nilsson (2004) — Traffic Safety Dimensions and the Power Model to Describe the Effect of Speed on Safety
`Nilsson_2004_TrafficSafety_PowerModel.pdf`
*Doctoral Thesis, Lund University, Bulletin 221*

- The foundational reference for the **Power Model** — the theoretical and empirical basis for quantifying the speed–safety relationship.
- Core equations (if mean speed changes from v₁ to v₂):
  - **Injury accidents**: A₂ = A₁ × (v₂/v₁)²
  - **Fatal accidents**: F₂ = F₁ × (v₂/v₁)³
  - **Fatalities**: D₂ = D₁ × (v₂/v₁)⁴
- The Power Model was **validated cross-sectionally** and confirmed for injury accidents and fatal accidents; for fatalities the model **underestimates** the effect — i.e., the true fatality increase with speed is even larger than the 4th-power predicts.
- Validated using Swedish speed limit experiments from 1967 onwards, and international data.
- The model implies: **a 10% speed increase → ~46% increase in fatalities** (1.1⁴ ≈ 1.46).
- Directly applicable to the unlimited Autobahn question: sections with no speed limit allow speeds well above 130 km/h, predicting substantially higher fatality rates than equivalent speed-limited sections.

## ⚠️ Elvik (2009) — The Power Model of the relationship between speed and road safety: Update and new analyses
*(TØI Report 1034/2009 — not downloaded, PDF unavailable)*

- Updated the Nilsson exponents using a larger dataset of international before-after studies.
- Confirmed the power model's validity; found the exponents are robust across road types and countries.
- Key update: the exponent for fatalities may be **higher than 4** in some contexts, reinforcing that Nilsson's model is conservative.
- Concluded that the Power Model remains the best available tool for predicting speed–safety effects.

## ⚠️ Elvik (2016) — An updated exponent for the Power Model
*(Accident Analysis & Prevention, 87, 177–185 — not downloaded)*

- Further update to the Power Model exponents using meta-analysis.
- Found that the relationship between speed and crash outcomes varies somewhat by road type and crash type, but the fundamental power relationship holds.
- Recommended exponents: injuries ≈ 2, fatal accidents ≈ 3, fatalities ≈ 4–4.5.

## ⚠️ Bauernschuster & Traxler (2021) — Tempolimit 130 auf Autobahnen: Eine evidenzbasierte Diskussion
*(Perspektiven der Wirtschaftspolitik, 22(1) — paywalled, not downloaded)*

- German-language policy paper reviewing evidence for/against a 130 km/h speed limit on all Autobahn sections.
- Concluded that the evidence strongly favours introducing a general limit of 130 km/h.
- Estimated that a 130 km/h limit would reduce **fatalities by ~140 per year** in Germany.
- Also calculated CO₂ and fuel consumption reductions as additional benefits.
- Reviewed the literature on speed and safety and found no credible evidence that unlimited sections are as safe as limited sections.

## ⚠️ Kloeden et al. (2002) — Reanalysis of travelling speed and the risk of crash involvement in Adelaide
*(Australian Transport Safety Bureau — not downloaded)*

- Case-control study on urban and rural roads in South Australia.
- Found **exponential increase in crash risk** with speed above the mean traffic speed.
- On 60 km/h roads: crash risk doubles for every ~5 km/h above mean speed.
- Widely cited as empirical confirmation of the individual-vehicle speed–crash risk relationship.

## ⚠️ Vadeby & Forsman (2019) — Traffic safety effects of new speed limits in Sweden
*(Accident Analysis & Prevention, 114, 34–39 — not downloaded)*

- Natural experiment: Sweden changed speed limits on many road sections in 2008 (both increases and decreases).
- Found that speed limit reductions led to significant crash reductions, consistent with the Power Model predictions.
- Speed limit increases led to crash increases, also consistent.
- Provides real-world validation of the Power Model in a large-scale national policy context.

## ⚠️ Stipdonk & Reurings (2014) — The effect of a speed limit increase from 120 to 130 km/h on Dutch motorways
*(Accident Analysis & Prevention, 73, 85–91 — not downloaded)*

- Natural experiment: Netherlands raised motorway speed limit from 120 to 130 km/h in 2012.
- Found a **statistically significant increase in injury accidents** on sections where the limit was raised.
- Estimated ~10% increase in injury crashes on affected sections.
- Directly relevant comparison: NL motorway data showing that even within the 120–130 km/h range, higher speed increases crashes.

## ⚠️ Wegman, Aarts & Bax (2008) — Advancing sustainable safety: National road safety outlook for The Netherlands 2005–2020
*(Safety Science, 46(2), 323–343 — not downloaded)*

- Overview of the Dutch Sustainable Safety programme and its outcomes.
- Netherlands achieved dramatic reductions in road fatalities through systematic approach: speed limits, infrastructure design, enforcement.
- Motorway safety in NL explicitly linked to speed limit policies and their enforcement.
- Provides institutional context for why NL motorway safety is strong relative to unrestricted Autobahn.
